# Project Leadership — Behavioral (STAR) Questions

[← Topic overview](../README.md)

> Topic: Driving ambiguous projects, cross-team coordination.

Senior STAR prompts. Question + **what good looks like** + concise sample answer.

---

### B1. Tell me about an ambiguous project you drove from scratch.

**What good looks like:** Manufacturing clarity (goal, metric, scope, plan), de-risking early, owning the outcome, and a measured result.

**Sample (STAR):**
- **S:** Leadership wanted us to "improve onboarding conversion" — no spec, no metric, no dedicated team.
- **T:** Turn that into a shipped, measurable improvement.
- **A:** I first defined success: a target activation rate, with a baseline I pulled from analytics. I mapped where users dropped off, wrote a one-pager with scope in/out and a milestone plan, and got sponsor sign-off. I spiked the riskiest assumption (which drop-off was biggest), then drove vertical slices, pulling in two contributors I didn't manage by aligning them to the goal.
- **R:** We lifted activation by a measurable double-digit percent against the baseline. The win came from defining the metric first and attacking the biggest measured drop-off, not from guessing.

### B2. Tell me about a time you led people who didn't report to you.

**What good looks like:** Influence through clarity and credibility, aligning to shared goals, making others' jobs easier.

**Sample:** "I led a migration spanning three teams with no authority over any of them. I led by making the plan compelling and concrete, reducing each team's overhead with clear interface contracts and ready-made tooling, and tying the work to a goal they all cared about. When one team's priorities competed, I escalated with data framed around the shared outcome. It delivered because I made it easy and aligned to follow, not because I could tell anyone what to do."

---

### B3. Describe a cross-team project and how you managed the dependencies.

**What good looks like:** Owning the seams, explicit contracts and dependency dates, surfacing slips early.

**Sample:** "Our feature needed work from the platform and data teams. The classic failure is integrating at the end, so I owned the seams: I defined interface contracts up front, assigned an owner and date to each dependency, set up a biweekly cross-team sync, and built a thin end-to-end integration early to flush out mismatches. When the data team slipped, the sync caught it weeks early and we re-sequenced calmly instead of hitting a wall at the deadline."

---

### B4. Tell me about a time a project was at risk of missing its deadline.

**What good looks like:** Critical-path awareness, early re-baselining, scope cuts over heroics, escalation with data.

**Sample:** "Midway through a launch project, the critical path slipped due to an underestimated migration. Instead of hoping or crunching, I quantified the gap, separated must-haves from nice-to-haves, and brought sponsors two options: cut scope to hold the date or slip two weeks for everything. I recommended cutting scope. We shipped the must-haves on time, deferred the rest to a fast-follow, and protected both quality and the team."

---

### B5. Tell me about a project that failed and what you took from it.

**What good looks like:** Extreme ownership, blameless root-cause, specific lessons applied later.

**Sample:** "A platform project I led missed its goal — we under-scoped a cross-team dependency and surfaced the risk too late. I owned it: in the retro I focused on what I could have done — a risk register and earlier escalation would have caught it. I didn't blame the other team. We salvaged the usable parts and re-planned. On the next project I instituted explicit seam ownership and weekly risk surfacing, and it shipped clean. The failure made me a better lead."

---

### B6. Tell me about driving the unglamorous last 20% of a project.

**What good looks like:** Owning the landing — integration, edge cases, rollout — and not declaring victory at "feature complete."

**Sample:** "A feature was 'done' but had been 90% done for weeks — momentum had died on integration, edge cases, and rollout. I reset the framing that feature-complete isn't done, made the remaining work visible with owners and a definition of done, took the gnarliest unowned bug myself, and drove a real staged rollout with metrics. We landed it properly, and I measured against the original success metric before calling it done."

---

### B7. Tell me about a time you delegated significant ownership.

**What good looks like:** Delegating ownership (not just tasks) with context, supporting without micromanaging, growing someone.

**Sample:** "On a project with a tight critical path, I was tempted to own the core component myself, but I handed it to a mid-level engineer with full context, a clear outcome, and my availability as backup. I resisted micromanaging — reviewed at milestones, not keystrokes. They delivered it and grew visibly, and it freed me to own the seams and unblock the rest. The project moved faster than if I'd bottlenecked it through myself."

---

### B8. How do you keep stakeholders and the team aligned through a long project?

**What good looks like:** Cadence, risk-forward status, re-baselining, protecting the team from thrash, no surprises.

**Sample:** "I run a regular written status that leads with risks and decisions, not just progress, so sponsors are never surprised and the team has a single source of truth. I re-baseline openly the moment scope or timeline shifts, and I absorb and filter external noise so the team isn't thrashed by every change in priority. That combination — proactive risk surfacing plus shielding the team — is what kept a recent 6-month effort calm and on track."
